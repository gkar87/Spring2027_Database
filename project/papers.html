The papers

They are grouped by the week of the course they belong to, so you can read yours while the topic is fresh. Search for the title in the library's article search or on the web; most of them are also on the authors' or publishers' own pages. If you want a paper that is not here, email me its title by April 4 and say why.

The model and the language (weeks 1 to 4)

  1. E. F. Codd. "A Relational Model of Data for Large Shared Data Banks." Communications of the ACM 13(6), 1970. Where relations, keys and the algebra start. Short, and still readable.
  2. D. D. Chamberlin and R. F. Boyce. "SEQUEL: A Structured English Query Language." ACM SIGFIDET Workshop on Data Description, Access and Control, 1974. The first version of SQL, and what its designers thought it was for.

Storage, indexes and query plans (weeks 10 to 12)

  1. R. Bayer and E. McCreight. "Organization and Maintenance of Large Ordered Indexes." Acta Informatica 1(3), 1972. The B-tree. Heavy on analysis; read it for the idea and the cost argument.
  2. P. G. Selinger, M. M. Astrahan, D. D. Chamberlin, R. A. Lorie and T. G. Price. "Access Path Selection in a Relational Database Management System." ACM SIGMOD, 1979. Cost-based optimization: the plan EXPLAIN shows you comes from this paper.
  3. V. Leis, A. Gubichev, A. Mirchev, P. Boncz, A. Kemper and T. Neumann. "How Good Are Query Optimizers, Really?" Proceedings of the VLDB Endowment 9(3), 2015. Tests real optimizers, PostgreSQL among them, and finds where the estimates go wrong.

Transactions and recovery (weeks 13 and 14)

  1. J. N. Gray, R. A. Lorie, G. R. Putzolu and I. L. Traiger. "Granularity of Locks and Degrees of Consistency in a Shared Data Base." IFIP Working Conference on Modelling in Data Base Management Systems, 1976. Intention locks, and the first account of isolation levels.
  2. H. T. Kung and J. T. Robinson. "On Optimistic Methods for Concurrency Control." ACM Transactions on Database Systems 6(2), 1981. Do the work first, check for conflicts at commit.
  3. H. Berenson, P. Bernstein, J. Gray, J. Melton, E. O'Neil and P. O'Neil. "A Critique of ANSI SQL Isolation Levels." ACM SIGMOD, 1995. Why the standard's definitions fall short, and snapshot isolation.
  4. C. Mohan, D. Haderle, B. Lindsay, H. Pirahesh and P. Schwarz. "ARIES: A Transaction Recovery Method Supporting Fine-Granularity Locking and Partial Rollbacks Using Write-Ahead Logging." ACM Transactions on Database Systems 17(1), 1992. Long. Critique sections 1 to 3 and the restart algorithm; say which parts you read.

Whole systems (any week)

  1. M. Stonebraker and L. A. Rowe. "The Design of POSTGRES." ACM SIGMOD, 1986. The goals of the system you have used all semester. Which of them survived?
  2. J. M. Hellerstein, M. Stonebraker and J. Hamilton. "Architecture of a Database System." Foundations and Trends in Databases 1(2), 2007. A survey of over 100 pages. Pick two sections, say which, and critique those.

Beyond one relational server (week 15)

  1. M. Stonebraker and U. Çetintemel. "'One Size Fits All': An Idea Whose Time Has Come and Gone." IEEE International Conference on Data Engineering, 2005. The case for specialized engines. A good pick if your NoSQL feature raised the question.
  2. F. Chang, J. Dean, S. Ghemawat, W. C. Hsieh, D. A. Wallach, M. Burrows, T. Chandra, A. Fikes and R. E. Gruber. "Bigtable: A Distributed Storage System for Structured Data." USENIX OSDI, 2006. Rows, column families and timestamps, sorted and split across many machines.
  3. G. DeCandia, D. Hastorun, M. Jampani, G. Kakulapati, A. Lakshman, A. Pilchin, S. Sivasubramanian, P. Vosshall and W. Vogels. "Dynamo: Amazon's Highly Available Key-value Store." ACM SOSP, 2007. Gives up consistency on purpose, and says when that is acceptable.
  4. J. C. Corbett and others. "Spanner: Google's Globally-Distributed Database." USENIX OSDI, 2012. Brings transactions and SQL back, across data centers.
  5. A. Pavlo and M. Aslett. "What's Really New with NewSQL?" ACM SIGMOD Record 45(2), 2016. A short survey; pair it with one system it describes if you choose it.

How to read one

Read it three times, each time more slowly, as S. Keshav suggests in "How to Read a Paper" (ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review 37(3), 2007):

  1. Ten minutes: title, abstract, introduction, the headings and the conclusion. What problem, and what do they claim?
  2. An hour: the whole paper, skipping proofs. Mark every figure and example you could redo by hand.
  3. As long as it takes: the part your critique is about, until you could explain it at the board with an example of your own.

Older papers use older words: a "data bank" is a database, a "relation" may be drawn as a flat file, and SEQUEL's syntax is not quite SQL's. Translating them into this course's terms is part of the work.

What the two pages hold

Two pages, 11-point type, single-spaced, with the references allowed onto a third. Use these four headings:

  1. The problem and the claim (about a quarter): what was hard before this paper, and what the authors say they solved. In your words, not the abstract's.
  2. The idea, worked through (about a quarter): the central mechanism, shown on a small example of your own. Using the course's university schema, or your project's, is ideal.
  3. Does it hold up? (about a third): what the paper assumes, what its evidence shows and does not, and what has changed since it was written. Where does PostgreSQL follow it, and where does it do something else? Check the PostgreSQL documentation before you say so.
  4. One question (a few lines): the question you would ask the authors, and why it matters to someone building an application like yours.

Cite the paper, the PostgreSQL documentation pages you used, and anything else you read. Quote sparingly and mark every quotation.

Questions people usually ask

  • Can two teams choose the same paper? Yes. Write your own critique; they will read very differently.
  • Can an AI tool summarize the paper for me? No. The course's limited-use policy applies: you may ask one to explain a term or a piece of notation, but the reading and the writing are yours, and the walkthrough will show it.
  • The paper is long. Do I have to read all of it? For papers 9 and 11, no: say which sections you critique. For the others, yes.
  • Can I disagree with a famous paper? Please do, with evidence. Agreeing is fine too, if you say why.

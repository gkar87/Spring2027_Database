How to use this page

  • Boxes marked Algebra take relational algebra. The buttons above each one insert a symbol where your cursor is, or you can type the plain word for it from the table below.
  • Press Run, or Ctrl+Enter (Cmd+Enter on a Mac). You get the result, the expression tree with the number of tuples at each step, and Show the SQL this became.
  • Results are sets, as in the algebra: no duplicate tuples. Boxes marked SQL run plain PostgreSQL, where results are bags.
  • Exercises have a Check answer button that compares your result with the answer. Your solved exercises are remembered in this browser only.

Slide 7. Conditions combine with ∧, ∨ and ¬.

Slide 8. π keeps some attributes and, because the result is a set, each tuple once. Ten instructors, six departments.

Exercises

Write each answer in relational algebra, then press Check answer (or Ctrl+Shift+Enter). Attribute names don't matter, but the attributes must come in the order asked. Exercises 1 to 6 are for everyone; 7 and 8 are a stretch.

The titles of all courses worth 4 credits.

Hint

Select the rows first, then keep one attribute. Example 1.5 has the shape.

Show a solution
π[title](σ[credits = 4](course))

The name and total credits of every student with at least 100 credits.

Hint

"At least" is ≥, or >= if you type it.

Show a solution
π[name, tot_cred](σ[tot_cred ≥ 100](student))

The names of students who took CS-101 but never took CS-347.

Hint

Subtract the ids, not the names or whole enrollments: two enrollments are never equal tuples if their courses differ. Then join with student to get the names.

Show a solution
took101 ← π[id](σ[course_id = 'CS-101'](takes))
took347 ← π[id](σ[course_id = 'CS-347'](takes))
π[name](student ⋈ (took101 − took347))

The names of students who took both CS-101 and CS-347.

Hint

Why doesn't σ[course_id = 'CS-101' ∧ course_id = 'CS-347'](takes) work? One tuple can't have two course ids.

Show a solution
took101 ← π[id](σ[course_id = 'CS-101'](takes))
took347 ← π[id](σ[course_id = 'CS-347'](takes))
π[name](student ⋈ (took101 ∩ took347))

Each student's name with the building of their department.

Hint

student and department share exactly one attribute, so a natural join is safe here.

Show a solution
π[name, building](student ⋈ department)

Each student's name with the title of every course they took in Spring 2026. Watch out for the trap in example 3.4.

Hint

The answer has 9 tuples. If you get fewer, your join is also matching on dept_name.

Show a solution
spring ← σ[semester = 'Spring' ∧ year = 2026](takes)
π[name, title](student ⋈ spring ⋈ π[course_id, title](course))

The name of the highest-paid instructor, without any aggregate function.

Hint

Find every instructor who earns less than someone. Everyone else earns the most.

Show a solution
earnsLess ← π[i.name](σ[i.salary < j.salary](ρ[i](instructor) × ρ[j](instructor)))
π[name](instructor) − earnsLess

This assumes no two instructors share a name; with ids it would be safer.

The names of students who took every course offered by the Comp. Sci. department, in one line, with ÷.

Hint

Divide a relation of (id, course_id) pairs by a relation of course_ids. Project takes first, or its other attributes end up in the answer.

Show a solution
π[name](student ⋈ (π[id, course_id](takes) ÷ π[course_id](σ[dept_name = 'Comp. Sci.'](course))))

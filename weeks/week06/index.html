Why design before tables

A design is cheap to change on paper and expensive to change once data and code depend on it.

Database design moves in four steps

E-R model

Entity-relationship model: a picture of the things a database is about (entities), the facts about them (attributes), and how they are connected (relationships). Peter Chen, 1976.

  • The diagram is for people: users can check it before anything is built.
  • It maps to tables almost mechanically. Normal forms, next week, then catch what the diagram missed.

Two ways a design goes wrong

Redundancy: the same fact stored twice

Put the department's building in every instructor row, and moving Physics means updating every Physics instructor. Miss one, and the database disagrees with itself.

Incompleteness: a fact with nowhere to go

With only that table, a new department with no instructors yet has no row to hold its building. A course nobody teaches has nowhere to exist.

The fix for both is the same: one table per kind of thing, linked by keys. The E-R diagram is how we find the kinds of things.

Entities and attributes

The things the database is about, and the facts it keeps about each.

An entity set is a set of things of one kind

  • Entity: one thing that can be told apart from all others. Avery Chen. CS-347.
  • Entity set: all entities of one kind, described by the same attributes. instructor, course.
  • Attribute: a property every entity of the set has. Each has a domain, its allowed values.
  • Key: attributes whose values identify one entity. Drawn underlined.
!

An entity set becomes a table, an entity a row, an attribute a column. The words differ because the diagram comes first.

How to find them in requirements

Read the users' description and mark the nouns. "Each instructor belongs to a department and teaches courses; a student takes courses and has an advisor."

Nouns that have facts of their own (a department has a building and a budget) are entity sets. Nouns that are only a value (a building name) are attributes. Verbs between nouns are relationships.

Attributes come in several kinds

Relationships

How entities are connected, how many of each, and whether every entity must take part.

A relationship set is a set of pairs

A relationship associates entities; a relationship set holds all of one kind. Here: no student has two lines, Jordan Patel and Emery Walsh have none. Those two facts are the next two slides.

Mapping cardinality: how many on each side

  • advisor is many-to-one from student to instructor: many students, one advisor each.
  • takes is many-to-many: a student takes many courses, a course has many students.
?

Cardinality is a business rule, not a fact about today's data. Ask the users: can a student ever have two advisors?

Arrows mean one; double lines mean every

  • Arrow toward an entity set: each entity on the other side is related to at most one of these.
  • Double line: total participation, every entity of that set takes part at least once. A single line is partial.
  • Limits l..h on a line: each entity of that set takes part at least l and at most h times. * means no limit.
  • 1..1 next to student is "exactly one advisor": arrow and double line in one label.

Relationships can have attributes, and roles

Ask where a fact belongs. A grade is not a property of the student (they have many) or the course (it has many), but of the pair: the relationship.

A relationship between an entity set and itself is recursive. Roles say which end is which; they become the column names course_id and prereq_id.

Some relationships join three entity sets

Three binary relationships (instructor–student, student–project, instructor–project) would not say which instructor guides a student on which project, when a student has two projects and two guides.

!

Use a ternary relationship only when the triple is the fact. Most relationships are binary; at most one arrow on a non-binary relationship, or it becomes ambiguous.

A weak entity set borrows its identity from an owner

  • Weak entity set: its own attributes cannot identify an entity. Double rectangle.
  • Identifying relationship: links it to its owner. Double diamond, total participation, at most one owner.
  • Discriminator: tells apart the weak entities of one owner. Dashed underline.
  • Key = owner's key + discriminator. Deleting the owner deletes them: ON DELETE CASCADE.

The same diagram in crow's foot notation

  • No diamonds: a relationship is a line; many-to-many is drawn as a table in the middle.
  • Each end has two marks: inner is the minimum (o zero, | one), outer the maximum (| one, crow's foot many).

For your project

Either notation is fine. Pick one, say which in your report, and use it consistently.

Extended E-R: specialization and aggregation

Kinds of entity inside an entity set, and relationships about relationships.

Specialization: subgroups with attributes of their own

  • Specialization is top-down; generalization is bottom-up.
  • Lower sets inherit the attributes and relationships of higher ones.
  • Overlapping or disjoint: can an entity be in two subgroups?
  • Total or partial: must every entity be in some subgroup?

Aggregation: a relationship that takes part in another

Without aggregation we would need a four-way relationship among instructor, student, project and evaluation, which repeats every triple. Aggregation lets the triple act as a single entity. In tables, eval_for holds the key of proj_guide plus the evaluation's key.

From diagram to tables

Each construct has a standard mapping. Apply them in order and the schema falls out.

Entity sets and their attributes become tables

-- composite name: one column per part
CREATE TABLE instructor (
  id             char(5) PRIMARY KEY,
  first_name     varchar(20) NOT NULL,
  middle_initial char(1),
  last_name      varchar(20) NOT NULL,
  date_of_birth  date
);

-- multivalued phone_number: its own table
CREATE TABLE inst_phone (
  id           char(5) REFERENCES instructor
                 ON DELETE CASCADE,
  phone_number varchar(15),
  PRIMARY KEY (id, phone_number)
);
  • Strong entity set: a table with its attributes; its key is the primary key.
  • Composite: keep the leaves (first_name, …), drop the parent name.
  • Multivalued: a table of (owner key, value), keyed on both. One row per phone number.
  • Derived (age): do not store it; compute it in a query or a view: age(date_of_birth).

A weak entity set: the owner's key joins the primary key

CREATE TABLE section (
  course_id  varchar(8)
             REFERENCES course ON DELETE CASCADE,
  sec_id     varchar(8),
  semester   varchar(6),
  year       integer,
  room       varchar(20),
  PRIMARY KEY (course_id, sec_id, semester, year)
);

-- takes then refers to a section, not a course
--   FOREIGN KEY (course_id, sec_id, semester, year)
--     REFERENCES section
  • The identifying relationship sec_course gets no table of its own: the foreign key in section is the relationship.
  • CASCADE, because a section cannot outlive its course.
  • A table that refers to a weak entity uses the whole key: four columns here.

Design choices, and a design studio

The rules map a diagram to tables. Choosing the diagram is the judgment part.

Three questions designers ask

  1. 1
    Entity or attribute?

    If the thing has facts of its own, or several per owner, or is shared, make it an entity. A phone number stays an attribute; a department, with a building and a budget, is an entity.

  2. 2
    Entity or relationship?

    An enrollment can be the relationship takes, or an entity registration that student and section relate to. Make it an entity when other things refer to it: an invoice per registration, say.

  3. 3
    Binary or n-ary?

    Prefer binary. Use three-way only when the triple is the fact, as in proj_guide.

!

The most common mistake: putting a foreign key as an attribute of an entity, such as dept_name inside instructor. In the diagram that link is a relationship. It becomes a column only when you map it.

Design studio: critique a first draft

The library: patrons borrow copies of books; a book can have several copies and several authors; a copy is out to at most one patron at a time; each loan has a date and a due date.

Five problems in the first draft

  1. book_isbn, book_title in patron: redundant, and only one loan fits. They belong to the relationship.
  2. due_date in patron: it describes a loan, so it is an attribute of the loan.
  3. The library lends copies, not books: copy is a weak entity of book, with copy_no.
  4. authors in book: an author has a name and writes many books: an entity, many-to-many.
  5. borrows is many-to-many: a copy is out to at most one patron, so loan is many-to-one to patron.

What to take away

  1. 1
    Design on paper first; tables follow.

    Entity sets become tables, relationships become keys.

  2. 2
    Cardinality and participation are business rules.

    Ask the users. Arrows mean one, double lines mean every.

  3. 3
    Put each fact where it belongs.

    On an entity, or on the relationship it describes. No foreign keys as entity attributes.

  4. 4
    Weak entities borrow their owner's key.

    Their primary key is the owner's key plus a discriminator.

  5. 5
    Some rules need more than a diagram.

    Total participation in many-to-many needs a trigger; next week's normal forms catch what the diagram missed.

Part B: ten questions in SQL

Try each query in the playground's scratch pad, then copy it into lab3.sql under its comment. Write the number of rows it returned in lab3_answers.txt. Show exactly the columns asked for, in the order asked, and name a computed column the way the question does.

  1. Q1. For every department that offers courses: the department, the number of courses as courses, and their total credits as credits, sorted by department.

  2. Q2. For every term in takes: the year, the semester, and the number of different students enrolled as students, sorted by year and then semester. A student who took two courses that term counts once.

  3. Q3. The departments whose instructors' salaries add up to more than 150,000, with that total as total.

  4. Q4. Every instructor with the number of students they advise as advisees, most first, then by name. An instructor who advises nobody must appear with 0.

  5. Q5. The title and enrollment count of every course taken more often than the average course. Count enrollments per course in a WITH step, and average over the courses in that step.

  6. Q6. The names of students who took at least one course from outside their own department. Use EXISTS.

  7. Q7. The names of instructors paid more than every History instructor. Use ALL.

  8. Q8. The names of students who took every course that Avery Chen has taught. Use NOT EXISTS with EXCEPT, as on slide 15.

  9. Q9. The number of enrollments in each grade band, as band and enrollments, sorted by band. The bands are A, B, C and F (an A− is an A, a B+ is a B, and so on), and a course with no grade yet is in progress. Use CASE.

  10. Q10. The student or students with the most total credits in each department: department, name and total credits, sorted by department. If two students tie, show both. Leave out students with no department. Use rank() with PARTITION BY.

Part C: two short answers

Answer in lab3_answers.txt, two to four sentences each. Run the queries first; the numbers are part of the answer.

  1. W1. Both queries are meant to count the students who are not in Physics. Run them and give each number. Then explain the difference, naming the student who causes it and what <> does with a NULL.

    SELECT count(*) FROM student WHERE dept_name <> 'Physics';

SELECT (SELECT count(*) FROM student)
     - (SELECT count(*) FROM student WHERE dept_name = 'Physics');

  2. W2. The department gives an award to its "top two" advisors, by number of advisees. Run the query below. If you kept the rows where each of rk, drk and rn is at most 2, how many advisors would get the award each time? Say which one you would use and why, and what is wrong with the other two here.

    WITH c AS (
  SELECT i.name, count(a.s_id) AS n
  FROM instructor i LEFT JOIN advisor a ON a.i_id = i.id
  GROUP BY i.id, i.name)
SELECT name, n,
       rank()       OVER (ORDER BY n DESC) AS rk,
       dense_rank() OVER (ORDER BY n DESC) AS drk,
       row_number() OVER (ORDER BY n DESC) AS rn
FROM c ORDER BY rn;

If something goes wrong

  • "column … must appear in the GROUP BY clause or be used in an aggregate function": a column in SELECT is neither grouped nor inside an aggregate (slide 7). Add it to GROUP BY, or wrap it in an aggregate.
  • "aggregate functions are not allowed in WHERE": a condition on a group belongs in HAVING (slide 8).
  • "window functions are not allowed in WHERE": compute the rank in a WITH step, then filter on it in the outer query (slide 23).
  • "more than one row returned by a subquery used as an expression": a subquery after = or > returned a list. Use IN, ALL or SOME, or make it return one value (slides 11 and 12).
  • A count that is too high after a join: count(*) counts the padded row too. Count a column from the right-hand table (slide 9), or use count(DISTINCT …) where the question says "different".
  • Anything else: copy the exact message and bring it to office hours, Tuesday and Wednesday 12:00–3:00 p.m., MIC room D159, or post it in the week 4 discussion in Brightspace.
